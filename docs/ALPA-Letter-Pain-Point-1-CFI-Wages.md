# PSA → ALPA: Pain Point 1 — The CFI Wage Trap
*Simulated probe letter to surface deeper data on Certified Flight Instructor compensation and attrition.*

---

**From:** Benjamin Bowler, Executive Committee, Pilot Shortage Association  
**To:** Capt. Jason Ambrosi, President, Air Line Pilots Association  
**Date:** June 1, 2026  
**Re:** Pain Point 1 — The CFI Wage Trap

---

Capt. Ambrosi,

In our last exchange, you stated that ALPA is "not a consumer protection agency" and that CFI wages are not ALPA's jurisdiction because CFIs operate under Part 61 and Part 141, not Part 121.

We accept that ALPA does not represent CFIs contractually. What we do not accept is ALPA's refusal to acknowledge the financial destruction this wage trap inflicts on the very pilots ALPA claims to protect.

This letter is not an attack. It is a data request. We want to understand ALPA's position with specificity so we can either align with it or challenge it on the facts.

---

## Question 1: What is ALPA's official position on CFI compensation?

You stated that ALPA "does not set CFI wages; corporate flight academies do."

We are asking for clarification:

- Does ALPA have an official policy statement — either from the Executive Board or the Master Executive Council — on what constitutes a living wage for flight instructors?
- Has ALPA ever surveyed its own membership to ask what they earned as CFIs, how long they stayed in instructing, and what financial sacrifices they made during that period?
- If ALPA has no official position on CFI wages, does ALPA consider the $30,000–$35,000 national average acceptable, unacceptable, or irrelevant to ALPA's mission?

We ask because ALPA's public messaging consistently states that ALPA "fights for the next generation of aviators." If the next generation is spending 2–4 years earning poverty wages while building hours, we would like to understand how ALPA squares that with its mission.

---

## Question 2: What data does ALPA have on CFI attrition?

You claimed that the civilian pipeline is a "highly secure conveyor belt of talent" and that the gap between CPL and airline hire is merely "standard incubation."

We would like to see the data behind that claim:

- How many CPL holders issued annually between 2015–2025 never reached an ATP or an airline interview?
- Of those who became CFIs, what percentage attritioned out of aviation entirely before reaching 1,500 hours — and what were the primary reasons cited (financial, medical, family, career change)?
- How many CFIs who started instructing in 2015, 2016, 2017, 2018, and 2019 are still instructing today versus how many reached an airline flight deck?

If ALPA does not collect this data, we ask: why not? If the pipeline is as healthy as ALPA claims, would not the data prove it conclusively?

We have our own data — informal, collected from pilot forums, ATO partnerships, and our own membership intake forms — suggesting that 40–60% of CPL holders who become CFIs attrition out of aviation before reaching 1,500 hours. We would welcome ALPA's counter-data. If ALPA has none, we ask ALPA to explain why it defends a pipeline it has never measured.

---

## Question 3: Why has ALPA never organized CFIs?

You stated that ALPA is "a Part 121 and Part 129 airline pilot union" and therefore cannot represent CFIs.

We are aware of the structural limitation. Our question is different: Has ALPA ever *tried*?

- Has ALPA ever conducted an organizing drive at a major Part 141 flight academy?
- Has ALPA ever filed for representation at a university aviation program where CFIs are effectively university employees?
- Has ALPA ever explored a separate affiliate or associate membership tier for CFIs and low-hour pilots — even without collective bargaining rights, simply for advocacy, aeromedical support, and career guidance?

We ask because other unions have successfully organized adjacent professions. The Screen Actors Guild represents background actors. The Writers Guild represents assistants. The Teamsters represent warehouse workers who never touch a truck. ALPA's claim that CFIs are simply "outside our jurisdiction" reads less as a structural impossibility and more as a strategic choice.

If ALPA has never attempted to organize CFIs, we ask ALPA to explain why the largest pilot union in the world has never tried to represent the largest population of working pilots in the country.

---

## Question 4: How does ALPA reconcile "protecting the next generation" with the CFI wage trap?

This is the central tension we are trying to understand.

ALPA's messaging:
- "We are fiercely protecting the next generation."
- "We ensure that when they finally sit in a commercial cockpit, they earn a lucrative, dignified wage."
- "Our door is wide open to every student pilot, every flight instructor, and every veteran aviator."

The reality for CFIs:
- $30,000/year (below the U.S. median individual income of ~$40,000)
- No health insurance at many Part 61 schools
- No retirement contributions
- No seniority, no union, no grievance procedure
- Required to maintain a Class 1 medical ($150–$300 every 6–12 months) out of pocket
- Required to maintain CFI certificate renewal ($100–$200 every 24 months) out of pocket
- Often required to pay for their own recurrent training
- Working 6–7 days per week during peak season, often without overtime pay
- Flying 800–1,000 hours per year in high-workload training environments with minimal rest protections

We do not expect ALPA to fix every problem in aviation. We do expect ALPA to acknowledge problems that directly affect the pilots ALPA claims to protect.

So the direct question: Does ALPA believe that a 25-year-old CPL holder with $80,000 in student debt who earns $30,000/year as a CFI while paying for their own medical, recurrent training, and certificate renewals is being "fiercely protected" by ALPA?

A yes-or-no answer will suffice. We will publish it either way.

---

## Question 5: What would ALPA support to fix the CFI wage trap?

We are not asking ALPA to negotiate CFI contracts. We are asking what policies ALPA would support — or at least not oppose — to address this pain point.

Specifically:

- Would ALPA support FAA regulations requiring Part 141 schools to publish CFI placement rates and average time-to-hire?
- Would ALPA support legislation requiring flight schools that charge over $50,000 in tuition to guarantee a minimum CFI starting wage of $45,000/year for graduates who become instructors?
- Would ALPA support a tax credit or student loan forgiveness program for CFIs who complete 1,000 hours of instruction and pass an ATP check-ride?
- Would ALPA support an industry-standard "CFI Bill of Rights" requiring schools to provide health insurance, retirement contributions, and paid recurrent training?
- Would ALPA support PSA's proposal for airlines to credit structured CFI time toward hiring preferences — recognizing that 1,000 hours of teaching instrument procedures is not equivalent to 1,000 hours of Part 135 cargo flying, but is still valuable experience?

If ALPA opposes any of these, we ask for the specific reasoning. If ALPA supports any of them, we ask ALPA to publicly state that support and work with us to advance it.

---

## Our Position

We are not asking ALPA to become something it is not. We are asking ALPA to stop pretending that the CFI wage trap is someone else's problem.

The CFI is not an incidental part of the pipeline. The CFI IS the pipeline. Without CFIs, no one builds hours. Without hours, no one reaches 1,500. Without 1,500, no one enters the flight deck ALPA claims to protect.

If ALPA genuinely believes that the pipeline is "healthy," "secure," and "conveying talent forward," then ALPA should have no objection to measuring it, publishing the data, and supporting reforms that make the conveyor belt less of a meat grinder.

If ALPA refuses to engage on this — if the response is again "not our jurisdiction" or "this discussion is over" — then the aviation community will draw its own conclusions about who ALPA actually protects.

We await your data.
