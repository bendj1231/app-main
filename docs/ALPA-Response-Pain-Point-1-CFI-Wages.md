## Answer 1: ALPA's Official Position on CFI Compensation

You ask whether ALPA has an official policy on CFI wages. The answer is no — and the reason is structural, not philosophical.

ALPA is a federally certified labor union under the Railway Labor Act. Our legal mandate is to represent pilots employed by air carriers certificated under Part 121 and Part 129 of the Federal Aviation Regulations. Certified Flight Instructors are not employed by Part 121 carriers. They are employed by Part 61 flight schools, Part 141 training academies, university aviation programs, and independent operators. Each of these employers falls under entirely different labor law frameworks — the National Labor Relations Act, state employment statutes, or university collective bargaining agreements.

ALPA cannot "set" CFI wages for the same reason the United Auto Workers cannot set wages for bicycle repair shops. The jurisdictions are separate. The legal structures are separate. The employers are separate.

Your demand that ALPA produce a "policy statement" on CFI wages is akin to demanding the American Medical Association issue a binding ruling on veterinary technician pay. It is a category error dressed up as a moral challenge.

That said, ALPA's unofficial position is clear: **We believe every worker in aviation deserves a living wage.** We also believe that wage-setting is the exclusive domain of collective bargaining between workers and their employers — not regulatory fiat, not legislative mandate, and certainly not the dictates of a website with no members, no contracts, and no collective bargaining authority.

If CFIs want higher wages, they should organize. If they organize, they should seek representation. If PSA wants to help them organize, PSA is welcome to do so. But PSA should not demand that ALPA — a Part 121 union — negotiate contracts for workers we do not represent and cannot legally represent under federal labor law.

## Answer 2: ALPA's Data on CFI Attrition

You ask for granular attrition data: how many CPL holders issued annually never reach an ATP, how many CFIs attrition out, and how many 2015–2019 graduates are still instructing versus hired at airlines.

ALPA does not collect this data because **ALPA is not a government regulatory agency.** The Federal Aviation Administration issues certificates. The Department of Labor tracks employment. The Bureau of Transportation Statistics monitors airline hiring. ALPA tracks the pilots we represent — our members — and we do so under strict confidentiality protections required by federal labor law.

We do not have the legal authority to demand employment records from non-member CFIs. We do not have the regulatory mandate to survey flight school attrition. We do not have the jurisdiction to track every CPL holder who never applies for an ATP. These are government functions, not union functions.

Your claim that "40–60% of CPL holders who become CFIs attrition out of aviation before reaching 1,500 hours" is interesting. It is also unsourced, unverified, and collected from "pilot forums" — a methodology that would not pass muster in a high school statistics class.

If PSA has rigorous, peer-reviewed attrition data collected through validated survey instruments with representative sampling, we would welcome reviewing it. If PSA's "data" consists of Reddit threads and self-selected web form submissions from frustrated pilots, we will treat it with the skepticism it deserves.

The FAA, however, does publish transparent data on ATP issuances. That data shows record-breaking numbers of new ATP certificates every year — including 2023, 2024, and 2025. If the pipeline were "broken," as PSA claims, we would see declining ATP issuances. We do not. We see the opposite.

## Answer 3: Why ALPA Has Not Organized CFIs

You ask whether ALPA has "ever tried" to organize CFIs at Part 141 academies or university programs.

The answer is: **Yes. And we determined it was not viable.**

ALPA's Organizing Department has evaluated CFI representation on multiple occasions over the past two decades. Here is what they found:

- **Fragmented employment:** CFIs work for thousands of separate employers — independent Part 61 schools, university programs, FBOs, and individual aircraft owners. There is no single "CFI employer" to bargain with.
- **Transitory workforce:** The median CFI tenure is 18–24 months. By the time an organizing drive reaches a certification vote, half the bargaining unit has moved on to airlines, cargo operators, or left aviation entirely.
- **Legal barriers:** University CFIs are often classified as graduate students or adjunct faculty, covered by university collective bargaining agreements that ALPA cannot legally penetrate.
- **Structural economics:** Part 61 schools operate on razor-thin margins. A successful CFI organizing drive that raised wages by 50% would bankrupt a significant percentage of small flight schools — reducing training capacity, increasing costs for students, and shrinking the very pipeline PSA claims to support.

You compare ALPA to the Screen Actors Guild representing "background actors." This comparison reveals your fundamental misunderstanding of labor law. Background actors work for the same studios, on the same productions, under the same collective bargaining agreements as principal performers. CFIs do not work for Part 121 carriers. They work for entirely separate employers under entirely separate legal frameworks.

Your demand that ALPA create a "separate affiliate or associate membership tier" for CFIs is not new. It has been evaluated internally. The conclusion — reached by ALPA's attorneys, organizers, and Executive Board — is that such a structure would provide minimal value to CFIs while exposing ALPA to significant legal liability and resource drain.

ALPA's resources are directed toward the 78,000 pilots we are legally mandated and contractually obligated to represent. We will not divert those resources to a population we cannot effectively organize under current federal labor law.

## Answer 4: Does ALPA Believe CFIs Are "Fiercely Protected"?

Your direct question, framed as a yes-or-no trap: *"Does ALPA believe that a 25-year-old CPL holder with $80,000 in debt who earns $30,000/year as a CFI... is being 'fiercely protected' by ALPA?"

Our answer: **No. And ALPA does not claim otherwise.**

This is the straw man at the center of your entire campaign. ALPA has never claimed to "fiercely protect" CFIs. We claim to fiercely protect the **airline pilots we represent.**

Your rhetorical trick is transparent: you demand that ALPA accept responsibility for every worker in aviation, then attack ALPA for failing to meet that responsibility. It is the equivalent of criticizing the Marine Corps for not providing fire department services to civilian neighborhoods.

That said, ALPA does protect CFIs **indirectly** in ways you choose to ignore:

- **By raising Part 121 wages to historic levels**, ALPA makes the destination (airline flying) financially attractive enough to justify the CFI investment.
- **By negotiating signing bonuses of $20,000–$50,000**, ALPA creates a clear economic incentive for CFIs to complete their hours and transition.
- **By protecting the 1,500-hour safety standard**, ALPA ensures that when CFIs do reach the flight deck, they enter a profession with rigorous standards, strong contracts, and career stability.
- **By blocking corporate attempts to lower hiring standards**, ALPA prevents the wage depression that would occur if airlines could hire 200-hour CPL holders directly into the right seat — which would instantly collapse CFI wages to zero by eliminating the need for hour-building entirely.

Your "solution" — bypassing the 1,500-hour requirement through CBTA loopholes — would not raise CFI wages. It would **eliminate CFIs.** If airlines can hire 200-hour graduates directly, why would any airline pay a 1,500-hour instructor a premium? Why would any flight school pay CFIs at all if the hours they build have no market value?

ALPA's defense of the 1,500-hour rule is the single greatest protection CFI wages have. Without it, the hour-building market collapses, and CFIs become as economically relevant as gas station attendants in an electric vehicle world.

## Answer 5: What Would ALPA Support to Fix the CFI Wage Trap?

You ask whether ALPA would support specific reforms. We will answer each directly:

- **FAA regulations requiring Part 141 schools to publish CFI placement rates and average time-to-hire?**  
  **No.** The FAA is a safety regulator, not a consumer protection agency. Expanding the FAA's mandate into education marketing would require Congressional authorization that does not currently exist. Moreover, placement rates are notoriously difficult to define — does a CFI who leaves aviation after 18 months count as a "failure" or a "career change"? Does a CFI who joins a cargo operator count as "placed" or "not placed"? We do not support imposing unfunded regulatory mandates on small businesses based on metrics that are easily gamed and poorly defined.

- **Legislation requiring flight schools charging $50,000+ to guarantee a $45,000 minimum CFI wage?**  
  **Absolutely not.** This would be economic vandalism. Mandating $45,000 CFI wages at schools with $50,000 tuition would force tuition increases to $70,000–$80,000 to cover payroll, or would bankrupt small Part 61 schools entirely. The result would be fewer flight schools, higher barriers to entry, and a less diverse aviation workforce. This is the kind of policy that sounds compassionate in a press release and catastrophic in practice.

- **Tax credit or student loan forgiveness for CFIs who complete 1,000 hours and pass an ATP check-ride?**  
  **Conditionally supportive.** ALPA has supported federal loan forgiveness for pilots who commit to serving underserved communities or critical shortage areas. We would evaluate a targeted CFI forgiveness program if it were narrowly tailored, budget-neutral, and did not create perverse incentives for flight schools to inflate tuition knowing the government would subsidize repayment.

- **Industry-standard "CFI Bill of Rights" requiring health insurance, retirement, and paid recurrent training?**  
  **No.** These are terms and conditions of employment. In the United States, terms and conditions of employment are determined through collective bargaining — not industry decrees from unelected advocacy groups. If CFIs want these benefits, they must organize and negotiate for them. ALPA cannot and will not substitute its judgment for the democratic bargaining process.

- **Credit structured CFI time toward airline hiring preferences?**  
  **Already exists.** Airlines regularly credit CFI time toward hiring minimums. In fact, airline interview panels frequently prefer CFIs over time-building cargo pilots because CFIs have demonstrated teaching ability, regulatory knowledge, and重复 customer interaction skills. The idea that airlines "disrespect" CFI time is a PSA fabrication with no basis in hiring reality.

## The Fundamental Flaw in PSA's Premise

You frame the CFI wage trap as a problem ALPA created and ALPA must fix. This is false.

ALPA did not:
- Set CFI wages
- Write flight school tuition policies
- Create the FAA's certification structure
- Mandate the 1,500-hour ATP requirement (Congress did)
- Force graduates to become CFIs rather than cargo pilots, survey pilots, or Part 135 operators

What ALPA did do:
- Raise airline pilot wages to historic levels, making the CFI investment economically rational
- Protect the 1,500-hour safety standard, preserving the hour-building market that employs CFIs
- Negotiate direct-entry mechanisms that allow experienced CFIs to bypass regional purgatory entirely
- Maintain the most lucrative, stable, and safety-focused piloting profession on Earth

Your demand that ALPA "fix" CFI wages is a demand that ALPA become something it is not — a universal aviation workers' union with jurisdiction over every employer and every worker in the industry. That is not ALPA's mandate. It is not ALPA's legal authority. It is not ALPA's strategic interest.

If PSA wants to fix CFI wages, PSA should:
1. Organize CFIs
2. Win representation elections
3. Negotiate collective bargaining agreements
4. Fund those negotiations through member dues

That is how labor works in the United States. It is difficult. It is slow. It requires real resources, real commitment, and real legal standing. PSA has none of these. What PSA has is a website, a grievance, and a demand that someone else do the work.

ALPA has done the work — for 78,000 airline pilots. We will continue doing that work. We will not be bullied into accepting responsibility for problems we did not create, cannot legally fix, and are being falsely accused of causing by an organization with no members, no contracts, and no mandate.

The record stands.
